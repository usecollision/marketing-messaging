## When to Use

Invoke when:
- Building a founder's or executive's authority in a niche over time
- Drafting a POV essay, LinkedIn article, keynote, or newsletter from original experience
- The brand publishes helpful educational content but nobody remembers who wrote it
- Preparing a contrarian take and needing it constructive instead of a hot take

## Workflow

### Step 1: Map Earned Insight

- **Earned insight** = a conclusion only this person can defend because of lived experience, proprietary data, or visible scar tissue
- Inventory sources — failed experiments, customer surprises, counterintuitive results, strongly held disagreements with industry practice, decisions that worked for a non-obvious reason
- Apply the outsider test to every candidate — could a competent stranger write this from public sources? If yes, it is content, not thought leadership

**Gate:** 5+ earned-insight candidates documented, each with the specific experience that backs it.

### Step 2: Choose Topics Where Insight Is Real

- Score candidates on — depth of earned insight, audience urgency, adjacency to the product (close enough to be relevant, far enough to be credible), and how crowded the existing conversation is
- Choose topics where the author has a genuine edge; skip topics where recognized experts already own the conversation
- Frame each topic as a belief shift — what the world believes versus what the author has observed. The shift is the product; the post is its packaging.

**Gate:** Topic shortlist of 3-5 with a one-line belief-shift framing each.

### Step 3: Structure the POV Essay

Default structure:
1. **The default belief** — stated fairly, even charitably. Steelman the position being challenged.
2. **Why it breaks** — the author's observation from experience that contradicts it
3. **The evidence** — specific data, story, or example from the author's own work. The scar tissue goes here.
4. **The reframe** — a new model or principle, named clearly enough to be quotable
5. **The implication** — what the reader should do differently on Monday
6. **The open loop** — invite the disagreement or pose the follow-up question

Rules:
- One essay, one idea — a second idea is a second essay
- Specifics over sweep — the author's numbers, customers, and mistakes, not generic claims
- The claim must be falsifiable — if no evidence could prove it wrong, it is an opinion, not a point of view
- Write in the founder's voice, not a corporate register — first person, active verbs, actual opinions

**Gate:** Outline approved with a named reframe and evidence mapped to every section.

### Step 4: Shape the Contrarian Take (When Used)

- Contrarian builds authority when it is earned and constructive — name the position being challenged, acknowledge why it is popular, then deliver the alternative with evidence
- Avoid contrarian for its own sake — pure negation buys attention and rents nothing; authority comes from the replacement model, not the attack
- Run the room test — would the author defend this, unchanged, in a room with the people being disagreed with? If not, rework.

**Gate:** The take passes the room test or has been reworked until it does.

### Step 5: Draft and Edit

- Draft in spoken language — thought leadership reads like thinking out loud, not like marketing
- Cut hedge words that dilute authority (maybe, sort of, I think) unless the hedge is the point
- Keep the founder's fingerprints — specific stories, names where permitted, real numbers
- Title formulas — "The [X] most people get wrong", "What [N] [events] taught me about [topic]", "We stopped [practice] and [result]", "The [industry] rule I was wrong about"

**Gate:** Draft complete; reads naturally aloud; the single idea survives a one-sentence summary test.

### Step 6: Plan Distribution

- Choose the primary surface per essay — newsletter, LinkedIn article, blog, talk, or podcast (podcast-appearances for placements)
- Repurpose through content-repurposing into threads, short posts, and follow-ups
- Seed before publishing — comment on adjacent conversations, share the question the essay answers, let the audience pre-commit attention
- Reply to every substantive comment in the first 24-48 hours (heuristic window — the reply quality, not the window, is what compounds)
- Cadence — one strong essay weekly beats five weak posts (heuristic); consistency is the strategy, not volume

**Gate:** Distribution plan with primary surface, repurposed formats, seeding steps, and reply commitment.

## Evaluation & QA

### Common Failure Modes

- Essays that summarize the industry instead of adding a point of view
- Contrarian takes that attack a strawman or sneer at the mainstream position
- Unearned opinions dressed as insight — no data, no story, no scar tissue behind the claim
- Corporate voice scrubbing away the founder's personality, making the piece interchangeable with competitors'
- Topics chosen for traffic instead of earned insight, producing me-too content that builds nothing
- No distribution plan, so the essay dies on one surface with zero reply engagement
- Several ideas per essay, diluting the reframe until it is no longer quotable
